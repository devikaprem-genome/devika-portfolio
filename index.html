NPAAC Standards

Overview of NPAAC & Why It Matters

The National Pathology Accreditation Advisory Council (NPAAC) sets the standards for the quality and safety of pathology services in Australia, including genomic testing laboratories. NATA accreditation ensures that laboratories comply with these standards throughout the testing process. For genome curation, understanding and applying NPAAC standards is critical, as errors at any stage can directly affect patient diagnosis and clinical management. I believe familiarity with these standards ensures curation follows a refined framework. This ensures that patients can receive results they can trust.

Pre-Analytical Processes

The pre-analytical phase includes all steps before laboratory processing begins. This stage is especially vulnerable to human error, such as incomplete request forms, sample mislabelling or mishandling, or incorrect patient demographics entered into LIMS. I understand that maintaining strict pre-analytical controls is critical because errors introduced here cannot always be detected or corrected downstream, and may result in delayed or incorrect diagnoses.

Analytical Processes

The analytical phase includes wet-lab and bioinformatics processing, ending with variant interpretation. A good analytical process will require a robust laboratory process, validated bioinformatics pipelines, and accurate variant-interpretation reasoning. Common errors such as mis-pipetting, use of expired reagents, indexing mix-ups, incorrect filtering thresholds, and misreading IGV data can introduce artefactual variants or result in true variants being filtered out or misclassified. I understand that NPAAC standards require validated, documented workflows at each analytical step to ensure reproducibility and accuracy.

Post-Analytical Processes

The post-analytical phase focuses on reporting and communication of results. Errors at this stage can have significant clinical consequences. Misapplying classification criteria in the final report, mistakes in clinical documentation, or unclear communication to clinicians can directly affect patient management decisions. I understand that the NPAAC standards address this phase by requiring documented review procedures for reports and clear communication protocols. An accurate and well-structured report can help lead to actionable clinical outcomes for the patient.

Genome Curation

Approach & Frameworks

Genome curation involves systematically evaluating genomic variants to determine their clinical significance. Accurate curation requires both a structured framework and biological reasoning. I base my approach on the ACMG/AMP guidelines for germline variants and the ClinGen somatic oncogenicity framework for somatic variants.

I evaluate each variant systematically by first understanding its protein-level consequence and then reviewing each relevant evidence criterion before assigning a classification. Accurate documentation of evidence is as important as the classification itself, especially in clinical settings where results directly impact patient care.

Tools Used

  • ClinVar — used to review existing variant submissions
  • gnomAD — used to assess population allele frequency and gene constraint
  • Emedgene — used for variant prioritisation and filtering
  • DECIPHER — used to identify functional domains within a gene
  • SpliceAI — used to predict the impact of variants on splicing
  • IGV — used to visually inspect variant calls, assess read depth, identify strand bias, and distinguish true variants from sequencing artefacts
  • COSMIC — used in somatic curation contexts to evaluate recurrence of variant
  • Cancer Hotspots — used alongside COSMIC to assess recurrence of somatic variants across cancer types

Adaptability & Problem Solving

During my curation training, I worked with both germline and somatic variant contexts, each requiring distinct frameworks and reasoning. Germline curation involved applying ACMG/AMP criteria, focusing on inheritance patterns, population frequency, and Mendelian disease mechanisms. In contrast, somatic curation focused on cancer biology, interpreting VAF in relation to tumour purity, assessing oncogenicity with ClinGen frameworks, and considering clonal evolution instead of inherited risk.

Transitioning between these curation contexts required me to adapt both my tools and my approach to evaluating evidence. I learned to identify the appropriate framework for each case, adjust my criteria as needed, and avoid relying on a single mode of reasoning when a different biological context was required. I consider this adaptability essential in genome curation, where each variant may require a new interpretive approach, and the ability to recognize and respond to these shifts quickly is critical.
